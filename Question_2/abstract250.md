The number of retracted articles has risen in recent years. To explore this, we analyzed 2,047 retracted articles indexed in PubMed. Articles published before 2002 took significantly longer to retract (49.82 months) compared to those published after 2002 (23.82 months, p < 0.0001). This suggests that journals are now retracting articles more quickly than in the past.

To assess changes in scrutiny, we compared impact factor (IF) to time-to-retraction. High IF journals did retract more quickly, especially for articles retracted due to misconduct, but IF only explained 1% of the variance. Greater scrutiny in prominent journals may play a role but does not fully explain the increase in retractions.

Rare offences, like plagiarism, are more frequent and are retracted earlier, suggesting the criteria for retraction have been expanded, and the barrier to retraction has been lowered. This suggests changes in scientific integrity policies.

Retractions appear to be more broadly distributed recently than in the past, where retractions were concentrated to a few offenders. 63.1% of retracted papers were written by authors with one retraction from 1993 to 2012, compared to 46% from 1972 to 1992 (p < 0.001). Authors with multiple retractions did not have a proportional increase.

The rise in retractions reflects changes in both author and institutional behavior. Lower barriers to publication are evidenced by authors with a single retraction, while lower barriers to retraction are indicated by the shorter time-to-retraction and the expansion of retraction criteria. These findings demonstrate the importance of vigilance to maintain scientific integrity.
